Q1: Is the methodology sound?

Partly. The skeleton has good epistemic hygiene—explicit objective constraints, source-class discipline, and reliability metrics—but its equal-weight top-level structure and loose dimension boundaries are not defensible as a research priority model. The biggest risk is that it optimizes a plausible-looking composite rather than the stated lexicographic decision problem: reach the capability/reliability gate, then minimize time and cost.
arxiv

(a) Equal top-level weights

No: 0.20 each is not defensible without an explicit value model. Equal weighting is only appropriate if all five dimensions are genuinely commensurable, non-saturated, and equally marginal at the operating point. They are not.

Capability and reliability are constraints, not tradeable objectives. If either falls below its threshold, the project fails regardless of how cheap or fast it is. Treating them as 20% of a weighted score can therefore produce a “balanced” plan that violates a hard gate.

Time and cost are not naturally equal either. For a student in Tokyo using fixed 4xV100 hardware, marginal GPU-time cost may be low or zero after capital cost, while calendar time may dominate opportunity cost; for a funded lab, the reverse may hold. The weights encode an unstated economic assumption.

“Intervention levers” is not a performance dimension. It is a search/action space. Giving it equal objective weight double-counts its influence: a lever affects capability, time, cost, and reliability, and then receives separate credit for being a lever.

(b) Orthogonality of dimensions

No: the partition is not cleanly orthogonal. The dimensions overlap both conceptually and causally.

Overlap	Why it matters
Reliability vs. time	More validation runs, independent reruns, human review, and repeat experiments improve reliability but directly consume wall-clock time and compute.
Reliability vs. cost	Human review, larger evaluation sets, multiple seeds, and external benchmarking raise cost.
Capability vs. reliability	“Capability” measured by a local judge can itself be unreliable; a high score may reflect judge bias, prompt leakage, or task-specific overfitting rather than real capability.
Intervention levers vs. everything	Levers are inputs/policies; capability, time, cost, and reliability are outcomes. They belong on different levels of the causal graph.
Time vs. cost	On fixed hardware, compute time and monetary cost may be nearly collinear. Keeping both as separate equally weighted dimensions can double-count the same resource constraint.

The correct fix is not necessarily to delete dimensions, but to define a causal DAG: intervention levers are decision variables; capability, reliability, time, and cost are outcomes; reliability metrics are measurements of a latent property, not independent objectives. An ablation or intervention must specify what is held fixed, because otherwise the estimated “effect” can absorb prompt, judge, sampling, and implementation differences.

(c) S-a / S-b / S-c classification

The classification is sound as an uncertainty taxonomy, but incomplete as a decision policy. Reporting “unknown” or an interval for S-c is better than manufacturing a point estimate, but it can become under-commitment if the skeleton treats unknowns as terminal rather than as decisions about what evidence to buy.

Real weaknesses:

S-b versus S-c is not sharp enough. “Needs external source or controlled experiment” covers radically different evidentiary situations: a public benchmark number, a 15-sample local ablation, and a user-preference threshold are not the same class.

S-c should not merely be reported as unknown. It needs a decision rule: identify the threshold owner, collect a minimal preference/acceptance study, or define a provisional threshold with explicit sensitivity analysis.

Confidence 0–1 is not a probability. That is stated correctly, but the skeleton must then avoid using it in expected-value calculations, Bayesian updating, or weighted scores without a calibrated mapping. Otherwise “confidence 0.8” quietly becomes “80% probability.”

Source class does not establish validity. An S-a number can be computable but wrong if its input measurement, unit, denominator, or time basis is misdefined. The skeleton needs provenance and audit checks, not only computability labels.

Reliability metrics need operational definitions. Cohen’s kappa is appropriate for categorical inter-rater agreement, but it corrects for chance agreement and should be reported with a confidence interval; repeatability should specify the rerun conditions, metric, and Wilson interval’s estimand.
link.springer
+3

Bottom line: the epistemic labels are a good start; the missing piece is an evidence-acquisition policy that converts S-b/S-c unknowns into ranked experiments, with predeclared thresholds and stopping rules.

Why the correction matters

The original wording commits a treatment-label error: it assigns the effect of one contrast—prompt scaffolding, S0 versus S5—to a different treatment arm, the SM pipeline. Even if both involve structured generation, they are not interchangeable interventions. A prompt-level ablation changes the input/instruction condition; an SM-arm comparison tests a pipeline architecture or procedure conditional on its own prompt and implementation. Attributing the former to the latter conflates the intervention with the comparison.

This is material because downstream decisions are sensitive to effect size:

A +0.182 gain may justify prioritizing structured prompting, expanding the ablation, or investing in better prompt design.

A +0.025, nonsignificant SM effect should not justify prioritizing the SM pipeline, allocating more compute to it, or treating it as a proven causal lever.

If the objective is fastest reliable path under a capability/reliability gate, promoting +0.182 as an SM effect could cause the skeleton to select an arm whose real measured advantage is near zero.

Statistical and design caveats

The correction is necessary but not sufficient. The +0.182 result remains weak evidence for a large prompt effect:

A local judge creates evaluation risk. The judge may favor verbosity, structure, or surface features of S5; without blind human validation or an external benchmark, the +0.182 may partly measure judge preference rather than task capability.

“Ablation difference” is not automatically causal. It is causal only if assignment was randomized or otherwise exchangeable, the prompt change was isolated, decoding and task conditions were fixed, and the evaluation was blind where feasible. Ablations can implement interventions, but only when the changed factor and held-fixed factors are clearly specified.
