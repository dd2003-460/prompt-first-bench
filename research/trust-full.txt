                                                                                          A BSTRACT




arXiv:2611.10787v1 [stat.ML] 7 Nov 2026
                                                    Recursive self-improvement offers a promising path toward AI agents that improve
                                                    through experience by refining their own harnesses without retraining their underly-
                                                    ing large language models (LLMs). Making this self-improvement process reliable,
                                                    however, requires more than generating new updates: agents must decide which
                                                    changes are worth keeping. A greedy gate that accepts updates based solely on
                                                    higher validation scores can mistake an observed gain for genuine progress and
                                                    lead to performance degeneration, whereas overly conservative statistical gates
                                                    may reject beneficial updates and hinder performance improvement. We introduce
                                                    Trustworthy Recursive Updates with Statistical Testing (TRUST), a framework that
                                                    adapts an online multiple hypothesis testing procedure to control the rate of false
                                                    evolution across rounds, with an LLM analyzing historical context to help deter-
                                                    mine how much evidence is required to update the incumbent. Under interpretable
                                                    assumptions, we prove that TRUST limits the proportion of accepted updates
                                                    that result in unacceptable performance loss. We further derive a lower bound
                                                    on the final performance gain. Across the benchmarks HotpotQA, DROP, and
                                                    APPS with the models DeepSeek-V4-Flash and Qwen-3.8-Flash, TRUST reduces
                                                    the average ratio of accepted inferior updates from the greedy gate’s 45.59% to
                                                    2.78%, while achieving higher final scores than the state-of-the-art statistical gate
                                                    baselines for recursive self-improvement. These findings demonstrate that TRUST
                                                    strikes a balance between statistical reliability and performance gain in recursive
                                                    self-improvement.

through an e-process test in a single round instead of the entire recursive self-improvement process
(Shawn, 2026). However, controlling the probability of any false acceptance across rounds can make

key challenge, therefore, remains: retaining opportunities for useful improvement while controlling
acceptance errors across rounds under limited validation resources.
We introduce Trustworthy Recursive Updates with Statistical Testing (TRUST), a general statistical
acceptance framework. Motivated by false discovery rate (FDR) control (Benjamini & Hochberg,
1995; Xu & Ramdas, 2024; Zhang et al., 2025b), we define the false evolution rate (FER) as a safety
criterion for recursive self-improvement. FER measures the expected proportion of accepted updates
that cause an unacceptable performance loss, thereby quantifying the gate’s false acceptance risk. To
control FER, we formulate the sequential acceptance decisions as an online multiple testing problem.
We also use an LLM to analyze historical context and help the error budget allocator assign less
restrictive testing levels to promising candidates. We further draw on noninferiority testing to improve
statistical power by allowing a possible loss tolerance (Blackwelder, 1982; Da Silva et al., 2009;
Schumi & Wittes, 2011), and use overlap correction to account for bias from reusing validation
samples across rounds (Dwork et al., 2015; Rogers et al., 2016). Figure 2 summarizes how TRUST
makes recursive self-improvement trustworthy.
Our contributions. We make the following three main contributions: (a) Our framework builds on
online multiple hypothesis testing with interpretable parameters, including the error budget, loss toler-
ance, and overlap correction strength. It naturally incorporates LLM guidance based on diverse forms
of historical context. (b) We establish statistical guarantees under explicit assumptions, including FER
control, power consistency for improving candidates, and a lower bound on deployment gain. These
results connect hypothesis testing criteria to performance gain in recursive self-improvement. (c) We
evaluate TRUST in a closed-loop self-improvement harness across HotpotQA, DROP, and APPS
with DeepSeek-V4-Flash and Qwen-3.8-Flash. Averaged across these settings, TRUST achieves the
highest final gain of 24.84 pp. Compared with the greedy gate, TRUST reduces the mean FER0 from
45.59% to 2.78% and the mean maximum drawdown from 4.963 pp to 0.047 pp, demonstrating high
performance gains with few erroneous acceptances and small drawdowns.

2    R ELATED W ORK
Recursive self-improvement and agent harness. An agent harness includes tools, context man-
agement, and execution control (Shi et al., 2026). Recursive self-improvement agents use feedback to
improve various harness components. Promptbreeder evolves task prompts (Fernando et al., 2024).
Reflexion, ExpeL, and Voyager convert experience into reflections, insights, and reusable skills,
respectively (Shinn et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024). CoEvo-Mem improves memory
retrieval (Ye et al., 2026). AFlow and Flow refine agentic workflows (Zhang et al., 2025a; Niu et al.,
2025). AgentSquare defines a modular design space with planning, reasoning, tool use, and memory
components (Shang et al., 2025). These works focus on proposing effective candidates, while we
study whether a new candidate should be accepted to make recursive self-improvement trustworthy.

Additionally, we design a harness with diverse editable components in our experiments to evaluate
how different gates affect performance and risk.

Hypothesis testing methods. Multiple hypothesis testing procedures are primarily based on two
error criteria: the familywise error rate (FWER) and the FDR (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995; Wang
& Ramdas, 2022; Robertson et al., 2023). FWER control is generally more conservative than FDR
control. Many popular online FDR methods with p-values rely on independence across tests (Ramdas
et al., 2018; Robertson et al., 2023). This independence can be difficult to satisfy when agents evolve
based on past feedback. The e-value is an alternative test statistic with a betting interpretation (Shafer,
2021). The e-BH and e-LOND procedures allow arbitrary dependence among valid e-values while
controlling FDR (Wang & Ramdas, 2022; Xu & Ramdas, 2024). Drawing on e-GAI (Zhang et al.,
2025b) and contextual online testing (Chen & Kasiviswanathan, 2020), TRUST constructs an e-value
and allocates the testing levels with context. In RSI, SGM uses statistical tests to decide updates
to control FWER across rounds (Wu et al., 2025), while PACE uses an e-process to control the
probability of false acceptance in a single round (Shawn, 2026). TRUST provides FER control across
rounds and achieves higher final scores than these statistical baselines in our experiments.

The greedy gate accepts a candidate whenever the empirical score difference exceeds zero, making
the acceptance decision sensitive to sampling and execution randomness. We formulate candidate
acceptance as a hypothesis testing problem. In round t, we test
                                      Ht0 : ∆t ≤ −ε,            Ht1 : ∆t > −ε.                                             (5)
If the null hypothesis Ht0 is rejected, the selected candidate is accepted as the new incumbent.
Otherwise, the previous incumbent is retained.

control, power consistency to accept improvements, and a lower bound on the final gain.

6.1   M AIN GATE COMPARISON

Datasets, models and metrics. We evaluate the benchmarks HotpotQA (Yang et al., 2018), DROP
(Dua et al., 2019), and APPS (Hendrycks et al., 2021) with the models Qwen-3.8-Flash and DeepSeek-
V4-Flash. For each dataset, the validation sets contain a total of 200 examples, with 300 held-out
examples used only as the test set. We use additional 20 examples to construct the initial information

7                C ONCLUSION AND L IMITATIONS
TRUST applies online multiple hypothesis testing to control FER across rounds in self-improving
agents, with LLM guidance and interpretable parameters for adjusting gate behavior. Our theoretical
analysis and experiments show that the framework helps balance useful improvement and acceptance
error control under limited validation resources. It extends existing statistical methods and provides a
flexible statistical foundation for future work on recursive self-improvement. We discuss this point
further in Appendix A.2. Overall, we show that TRUST strikes a balance between statistical reliability
and performance gains in recursive self-improvement.
The present study does not address how to identify an exact value of overlap correction strength from
data or how to optimally tune other parameters. The noninferiority margin provides an interpretable
tolerance for loss, but the theoretical guarantee can be conservative and only partly explains practical
behavior. The LLM allocator can occasionally make mistakes when judging candidate updates, which
may lead to poor choices of LLM multipliers. Optimizing the gate to maximize gain while keeping
safety metrics within target ranges is also a promising direction. Future work can address these
limitations to make the recursive self-improvement process more trustworthy and effective.

A     S UPPLEMENTARY D ISCUSSION
A.1   I NTERPRETATION OF THE OVERLAP CORRECTION

We do not develop a complete model of validation data reuse in this work. Detailed studies of adaptive
data analysis can be found in prior work (Dwork et al., 2015; Rogers et al., 2016; Russo & Zou,
2016; Xu & Raginsky, 2017). Instead, we state sufficient conditions for valid e-values and focus on a
framework for FER control across RSI rounds. The interpretation of β is related to how historical
validation information, including feedback summarized in Ct , is used in candidate construction
and selection. Greater reliance on information from previously evaluated samples may increase
optimism when those samples are reused. For example, later rounds may use a few failed cases from
the previous round or summaries of common error types. These forms of feedback reveal different
information about the validation data and may lead to different degrees of optimism bias in subsequent
evaluation. The parameter β specifies an allowance for this optimism under the null hypothesis. A
stronger correction requires stronger validation results to reach the acceptance threshold, making
the gate more conservative and potentially limiting both false acceptances and the acceptance of
useful updates. Our sensitivity results in Section 6.2 and Appendix H support the practical value of
an appropriate correction strength. In the reported ablation, an appropriate β achieves a higher mean
final gain than no correction, whereas larger values make the gate overly conservative and reduce
final gains.

A.2   R ELATION TO E XISTING S TATISTICAL M ETHODS

TRUST adapts FDR control in multiple hypothesis testing to the acceptance of candidates in self-
evolving agents. It provides a framework covering the validation protocol, correction for data reuse,
noninferiority testing, construction of test statistics, and allocation of testing levels. Within this
framework, an LLM analyzes the candidate and historical context to guide allocation, while statistical
guarantees hold under the stated assumptions. Existing statistical studies (Zhang et al., 2025b; Chen
& Kasiviswanathan, 2020) compare testing procedures on specified hypotheses and observation
streams in their numerical experiments. In our RSI setting, the proposer constructs candidates using
historical information. Different gates can produce different acceptance decisions and incumbents,
leading to different histories and subsequent candidates. Our experiments follow this closed loop
and use update operators motivated by prior work on RSI agents. This allows us to compare how
different gates affect both acceptance decisions and subsequent search on real tasks. Our theoretical
analysis follows the same adaptive process and connects a lower bound on final gain to the testing
levels and other parameters of the gate. It thus provides a statistical foundation for analyzing both
acceptance risk and improvement along the trajectory induced by the gate.

This appendix describes the operators used in our experiments. Operator names remain fixed across

                                                        27
F.1   S TATE REPRESENTATION

Inspired by modular agent design and automated agent search in AgentSquare and ADAS (Shang
et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2025), we organize the mutable agent state into four editable components:
reasoning, routing, memory, and workflow. The four-component representation and the specific
operator definitions below are implementation choices of our harness.
The mutable harness state is
                                           H = (R, P, M, W),
where R is the reasoning strategy, P is the context router/provider, M is episodic memory, and W is
workflow. These components determine the instructions and context for a single solver call.
A proposed transition has the structured form
                          ot = (κt , operator, target, parameters),
where κt ∈ {R, P, M, W} identifies the component to modify; operator specifies the operation to
perform; target identifies the rule, memory entry, or configuration field to which the operation
applies; and parameters supplies the values or content required by that operation. For example,
refining an existing reasoning rule uses κt = R, operator=REFINE_RULE, the rule identifier as
target, and the revised procedure in parameters.

F.2   R EASONING OPERATORS

Motivated by prior work on modular reasoning, decomposition, self-refinement, and prompt scope
(Zhou et al., 2023; Madaan et al., 2023; Fernando et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024), we represent
the reasoning strategy as an explicit and editable rule set. The corresponding operators allow
reasoning rules and decomposition policies to be added, revised, generalized, reprioritized, or removed
based on closed historical feedback. The particular typed rule schema and operator definitions are
implementation choices of our harness.
The reasoning component stores the base analysis protocol, decomposition policy, and a bounded
collection of specialist rules. It is compiled into the mutable analysis instruction of the existing solver
and does not alter the fixed role prompt.

G.1    C OMMON PROTOCOL

We evaluate Greedy, PACE, SGM-B, SGM-H, and TRUST on HotpotQA, DROP, and APPS with
Qwen-3.8-Flash and DeepSeek-V4-Flash. Each method runs for 30 outer rounds. For HotpotQA,
DROP, and APPS, solver output seeds range from 0 to 4 and data split seeds range from 42 to 46.
Within each setting, all methods share the same initial test responses. Throughout the experiments,
we reuse cached responses for identical inputs whenever possible to ensure fair comparisons across
methods. One candidate is then selected for validation on up to 40 examples sampled without
replacement from the 200-example validation pool; validation samples may overlap across rounds.
The initial set contains 20 examples and the validation pool contains 200 examples. All four
components and 20 operators in Appendix F are available. Performance is evaluated on 300 held-out
test examples, which are used only for reporting.
In each setting, all gates start from the same initial harness, which contains prespecified weaknesses
that provide clear opportunities for improvement. This design allows us to examine whether different
gates accept candidates that correct these weaknesses. In later rounds, when the incumbent has
typically reached a higher level of performance, we also examine whether the gates reject subsequent
candidates that could reduce performance and cause the agent to lose earlier gains. The experiments
therefore examine different stages of the RSI process, from repairing initial weaknesses to preserving
improvements during continued search.
For API calls, we configure the generation parameters as follows. All roles use top-p = 1. The
temperature is set to 0 for the Solver and Allocator, and to 0.3 for the Proposer, except for HotpotQA
with DeepSeek, where the Proposer uses 0.7. The Solver’s maximum output length is 512, 768, and
8192 tokens for HotpotQA, DROP, and APPS, respectively, while the Allocator uses a uniform limit
of 4096 tokens. The Proposer’s maximum output length is 2400 tokens, except on HotpotQA, where
DeepSeek and Qwen use limits of 1536 and 2048 tokens, respectively.
