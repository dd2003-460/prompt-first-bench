# Architectural Evolution of Attention Heads: From Transformer Base to GPT-3 175B

## Executive Summary

The architecture of the Transformer, introduced in 2017, established the foundational paradigm for modern natural language processing by utilizing multi-head self-attention mechanisms. The original "Attention Is All You Need" paper defined specific head counts for its base and big models, which served as the baseline for subsequent scaling efforts. As the field progressed toward larger scale, such as OpenAI’s GPT-3 (175B parameters), the number of attention heads increased significantly to capture more complex dependencies within text. This report provides a detailed technical comparison of the head counts in the original Transformer base and big models versus the GPT-3 175B model. It further contextualizes these architectural choices by analyzing recent research on the functional roles, efficiency, and potential redundancy of attention heads, including phenomena such as attention collapse, inactive heads, and the computational implications of head pruning and fusion.

## Historical Baseline: The Original Transformer Architecture

The seminal work "Attention Is All You Need" (Vaswani et al., 2017) introduced the Transformer architecture, which relies on multi-head attention to allow the model to jointly attend to information from different representation subspaces at different positions. Although the provided sources do not contain the full text of the 2017 paper, they reference the "All-attention Transformer" and the general lineage of Transformer-based models that have shown impressive language modeling performance ([Shim et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.03252v1)). To answer the query regarding the specific head counts of the base and big models, it is necessary to rely on the standard specifications established in that foundational work, which are widely cited in subsequent literature discussing Transformer variants.

### Base Model Specifications
In the original Transformer architecture, the **Base** model was designed to be computationally efficient while maintaining strong performance. It utilized **8 attention heads**. The model had an embedding dimension ($d_{model}$) of 512, and each head had a dimension ($d_k$) of 64, calculated as $d_{model} / \text{number of heads}$ ($512 / 8 = 64$). This configuration allowed the model to process sequences with a context length of 512 tokens. The use of 8 heads represented a balance between parallelization and the complexity of the attention mechanism, enabling the model to learn different types of relationships (such as syntactic or semantic) simultaneously.

### Big Model Specifications
The **Big** model in the same paper scaled up the architecture to improve performance on machine translation tasks. It utilized **16 attention heads**. The embedding dimension was increased to 1024, resulting in a head dimension of 64 ($1024 / 16 = 64$). This doubling of heads compared to the base model allowed for a richer representation of the input space. The big model also featured a larger feed-forward network dimension ($d_{ff} = 4096$) and more layers (6 vs. 6, but with wider dimensions). The increase from 8 to 16 heads was a critical step in demonstrating that scaling the number of parallel attention pathways could yield significant improvements in perplexity and translation quality.

It is important to note that while the provided sources discuss various modifications to Transformer architectures, such as layer-wise pruning ([Shim et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.03252v1)) or the removal of attention entirely in favor of feed-forward layers for vision tasks ([Melas-Kyriazi, 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2105.02723v1)), the core concept of multi-head attention remains central to most large language models (LLMs). The original head counts of 8 and 16 serve as the historical baseline against which modern massive models are compared.

## Scaling to GPT-3: The 175B Parameter Model

As the field moved from the Transformer era to the Large Language Model era, the scale of these architectures increased exponentially. GPT-3, released by OpenAI in 2020, represents one of the first models to reach the 100-billion parameter range. The largest version of GPT-3 contains **175 billion parameters**.

### Head Count in GPT-3 175B
The GPT-3 175B model utilizes **96 attention heads**. This is a substantial increase from the 16 heads in the original Transformer Big model. The model has an embedding dimension of 12,288, and with 96 heads, each head operates on a dimension of 128 ($12,288 / 96 = 128$). This increase in head count is directly correlated with the increase in model width and depth. GPT-3 features 96 transformer layers, each containing 96 attention heads, resulting in a total of 9,216 individual attention heads across the entire network.

The rationale behind increasing the number of heads is to allow the model to attend to a wider variety of patterns and dependencies within the text. With 96 heads, the model can partition the representation space into many more subspaces than the original 8 or 16, potentially allowing for finer-grained distinctions between different linguistic features. However, this comes at a significant computational cost, as the attention mechanism scales quadratically with sequence length and linearly with the number of heads.

## Functional Analysis of Attention Heads in Modern LLMs

While the increase in head count from 16 to 96 suggests a proportional increase in capacity, recent research indicates that not all heads contribute equally to model performance. Understanding the functional diversity and potential redundancy of these heads is crucial for interpreting the significance of the head count difference.

### Inactive Heads and Computational Redundancy
A significant portion of attention heads in pretrained Large Language Models (LLMs) may be functionally redundant. Sandoval-Segura et al. (2025) conducted a comprehensive evaluation of 12 score functions to identify inactive heads in pretrained LLMs. Their findings revealed that **more than 12% of attention heads are inactive on average** across three model families ([Sandoval-Segura et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.03889v4)). These inactive heads can be ablated in specific contexts while maintaining MMLU accuracy to within 1% of the pretrained LLM. This suggests that while GPT-3 uses 96 heads per layer, a non-trivial fraction of these heads may not be contributing meaningfully to the final output in all scenarios.

The study further highlighted that relying solely on attention weights to identify inactive heads is insufficient. Score functions measuring the average norm of a head's output were more effective, consistently identifying inactive heads that weight-based methods missed ([Sandoval-Segura et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.03889v4)). This implies that the "effective" head count in a model like GPT-3 might be lower than the nominal 96, challenging the assumption that more heads always equate to better performance without careful architectural design.

### Attention Collapse Pathology
Another critical issue affecting attention heads, particularly in models using certain positional encodings, is attention collapse. Schallon (2026) identified a systematic pathology in the BLOOM family of transformer language models, where ALiBi (Linear Biased) positional encoding causes **31-44% of attention heads** to attend almost entirely to the beginning-of-sequence token ([Schallon, 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.09616v1)). This collapse concentrates in head indices where ALiBi's slope schedule imposes the steepest distance penalties.

Although GPT-3 does not use ALiBi (it uses learned positional embeddings), the phenomenon of heads becoming specialized or "stuck" in specific behaviors is relevant. Schallon’s work demonstrates that surgical reinitialization of collapsed heads can recover operational head capacity, recovering 98.7% of head functionality in BLOOM-1b7 ([Schallon, 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.09616v1)). This underscores that the nominal head count is not always reflective of the *functional* head count, and that training dynamics can lead to significant inefficiencies if not monitored.

### Specialized Roles: Membership Testing and Induction
Not all heads perform generic attention; some specialize in specific computational tasks. Balogh (2026) identified attention heads that function as membership testers, answering whether a token has appeared before in the context ([Balogh, 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.17526v1)). In GPT-2 small, medium, and large, as well as Pythia-160M, certain heads (e.g., L0H1 and L0H5 in GPT-2 small) act as high-precision membership filters with false positive rates of 0-4%, even exceeding the capacity of classical Bloom filters ([Balogh, 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.17526v1)).

These membership-testing heads form a spectrum of strategies, with some showing classic Bloom filter capacity curves and others acting as general prefix-attention heads ([Balogh, 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.17526v1)). The presence of such specialized heads in smaller models like GPT-2 suggests that in larger models like GPT-3, the 96 heads likely contain a diverse ecosystem of specialized functions, including induction heads, previous-token heads, and membership testers. This functional diversity is a key advantage of having a higher head count, allowing the model to parallelize different cognitive tasks.

## Efficiency Implications: Pruning and Fusion

The high head count in models like GPT-3 presents both opportunities and challenges for efficiency. Shim et al. (2021) proposed layer-wise attention head pruning to reduce computation and parameters proportionally to the number of pruned heads ([Shim et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.03252v1)). They noted that traditional head pruning does not evenly reduce load because the heavy feedforward module remains unaffected ([Shim et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.03252v1)). By applying layer-wise pruning, their pruned model showed consistently lower perplexity than Transformer-XL within a comparable parameter size on WikiText-103 ([Shim et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.03252v1)).

This technique is highly relevant to GPT-3-scale models. If 12% of heads are inactive ([Sandoval-Segura et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.03889v4)), pruning these heads could significantly reduce inference costs without degrading performance. However, Usami et al. (2026) warn that fusing attention heads is not trivial. They proved that exact fusion of softmax attention heads can require exponentially many atoms to preserve the output, specifically showing that $d$ two-atom heads use $2d$ terms, whereas the minimum single-head representation has exactly $2^d$ atoms ([Usami et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.02943v1)). This exponential blowup means that simply merging heads to reduce count is computationally infeasible for preserving exact behavior, reinforcing the value of keeping distinct heads even if some are less active.

## Conclusion

The evolution from the original Transformer’s 8 and 16 heads to GPT-3’s 96 heads reflects the broader trend of scaling in deep learning. While the nominal head count increased sixfold from the Transformer Big model to GPT-3, the functional utility of these heads is nuanced. Recent research indicates that a significant percentage of heads in large models may be inactive or prone to collapse, suggesting that the "effective" head count is lower than the architectural specification. Furthermore, heads often specialize in distinct tasks, such as membership testing or induction, highlighting the importance of maintaining a diverse set of attention pathways.

The comparison reveals that while scaling head count improves the model's ability to capture complex dependencies, it also introduces challenges related to computational efficiency and training stability. Techniques such as layer-wise pruning and surgical reinitialization offer promising avenues for optimizing these architectures. Ultimately, the jump from 16 to 96 heads was a necessary step in achieving the capabilities of GPT-3, but future advancements may focus on maximizing the efficiency and specialization of each head rather than simply increasing their number.
